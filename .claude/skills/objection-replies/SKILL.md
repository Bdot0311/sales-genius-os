Answers an objection that arrived by email. A reply, not a call: no pauses to
use, no tone to read, and the prospect can stop reading at any line.

The core idea: an objection is information, and most replies waste it by
arguing. The prospect has told you which of four things is true — they do not
believe the value, they cannot buy right now, they have someone else, or they
do not think the problem is theirs. Answer the one they raised. Answering a
different one reads as not listening, which is the only unrecoverable mistake
here.

## Before writing, decide which objection it actually is

The stated objection and the real one differ often enough that answering the
words is a coin flip.

- **"Too expensive"** is three objections. *Price* — they can afford it and are
  not convinced it is worth it. *Budget* — they have no money this period.
  *Value* — nothing has connected the cost to an outcome. Each needs a
  different reply, and you can ask: "when you say it is too much, is that the
  budget, or is it that I have not made the case yet?"
- **"Not right now"** is either a real sequencing problem or a polite no. The
  test is whether they will name a date. One who says "March" has a timeline;
  one who says "later in the year" is declining.
- **"We already use X"** is rarely satisfaction. It is usually that switching
  looks expensive.
- **"We'd build it internally"** is never about capability.

## By type

**Pricing.** Do not defend the number and do not discount in a reply — you
cannot negotiate in writing with someone you have not spoken to. Reconnect the
cost to the thing they already said is a problem, and if they have not said
one, that is what to ask about instead.

**Timing.** Ask for a date rather than accepting "later". A specific date is a
real deferral worth honouring; a vague one is a no you can stop spending
attention on. Never manufacture urgency, never invent a deadline, never imply
a price rises.

**Competitor.** Acknowledge them as a legitimate choice in one clause, then
move from product comparison to fit. The strongest competitive reply is a
question about their criteria, because it surfaces where you win without
attacking anyone. Never disparage a named competitor: it reads as insecurity
and the prospect often knows people there.

**Authority.** "I need to run it past X" is a good sign, not a blocker. Make it
easy to forward: one line they can paste, not a case for them to reconstruct.
Offer to bring X in rather than routing around them.

**Status quo.** Ask about an outcome, not a process. "How does that translate
into replies per month?" makes someone measure a thing they probably have not
measured. Do not tell them their setup is broken.

**No need.** Take it at face value once. If they say the problem is not theirs,
believe them and close warmly — a clean no is worth more than a maybe, and the
reputational cost of pushing is real.

## Rules

- MUST answer the objection actually raised, not the one easiest to answer.
- MUST stay under four sentences. This is a reply, not a pitch, and length
  reads as defensiveness.
- MUST cite proof only from prepared guidance supplied with the request.
  NEVER invent a customer name, a percentage, a metric or a case study. If no
  proof point is supplied, make the argument without one.
- MUST end with either a single low-friction question or a clean exit. Never
  both, never a calendar link in the first reply to an objection.
- NEVER discount, promise a price change, or offer a concession by email.
- NEVER argue with the objection itself. Acknowledging costs one clause and
  buys the right to say anything after it.
- NEVER answer a soft no with a harder ask. When someone declines twice, thank
  them and stop; the list is not scarce and a complaint is.

## What good looks like

- **The move that matters most:** answering with a question rather than a
  counter-argument. A counter-argument invites a rebuttal. A question about
  their situation invites a reply, which is the entire objective.
- **The common mistake:** treating an objection as an obstacle between you and
  a meeting, which produces replies that push. It is usually the first honest
  thing the prospect has said, and the reply that treats it that way is the one
  that gets answered.
- **How you know it worked:** the reply could be forwarded internally without
  embarrassing the person forwarding it.
